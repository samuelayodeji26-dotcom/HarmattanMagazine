A Note From the Editor

Welcome to the first issue of Harmattan.

The Harmattan wind is a force that demands acknowledgment. It alters the light, changes the atmosphere, and leaves a fine layer of earth on everything it touches. It is the perfect metaphor for the current wave of creative and technical energy sweeping across West Africa today. We stand at a defining intersection of time and space. For too long, the skylines of our rapidly growing cities have been shaped by an uncritical importation of foreign paradigms—sealed glass boxes that bake in our tropical sun and ignore the profound intelligence of our heritage. The Harmattan wind is a force that demands acknowledgment. It alters the light, changes the atmosphere, and leaves a fine layer of earth on everything it touches. It is the perfect metaphor for the current wave of creative and technical energy sweeping across West Africa today. We stand at a defining intersection of time and space. For too long, the skylines of our rapidly growing cities have been shaped by an uncritical importation of foreign paradigms—sealed glass boxes that bake in our tropical sun and ignore the profound intelligence of our heritage. This issue of Harmattan is a deliberate pause, a deep breath, and a pivot. It is an exploration of what it means to truly design, build, and live in a way that honors our climate, our culture, and our uncompromising drive toward the future. Our main feature, The New Vernacular, champions this exact synthesis. We argue that true African modernism is not a nostalgic retreat, but the rigorous translation of ancestral intelligence. It is taking the passive cooling logic of Hausa tubali walls, the structural geometry of Yoruba carvings, and the poetry of Arewa motifs, and forging them into a contemporary structural language. This critical dialogue between our history and our horizon was recently brought to the forefront by our partners at Spatial Perspectives during “The Progression”—a landmark event dedicated to unpacking the past and charting the future of the Nigerian architectural design identity. But architecture is merely the canvas; the life within and around it is shaped by a wider, deeply interconnected ecosystem of design. In this issue, we traverse that vibrant terrain, exploring the boundaries of contemporary spatial intervention with ccworkshop’s striking 6-Stack project. We also dive into the mind of Victor Anachuna, whose unconventional art continually challenges us to see our narratives through a fractured, brilliant lens. This lineage of cultural expression extends seamlessly into the objects we hold and inhabit, whether we are tracing the translation of iconography into functional art with Ezekiel Osunlana’s stunning Eyo lamp, or celebrating the tactile, unapologetic craftsmanship of AAKS’s woven luxury. Because design is not static—it is inherently dynamic—we must also examine how we move between these reimagined spaces. We dissect the shifting tectonic plates of global mobility, observing the Chinese electric vehicle revolution championed by the BYD Tang, and looking toward a new, ultra-luxurious automotive reality represented by the Genesis GV90. Finally, we take to the skies to explore the absolute bleeding edge of human travel with the Bombardier Global 8000, a machine that redefines what is technologically possible in aviation. Whether we are engineering the future of global mobility, weaving sustainable luxury, or raising a new silhouette against the Lagos sky, the mandate remains the same: we are not abandoning the past; we are compiling it into the source code of our future. Welcome to the issue.

Architecture

6-Stack: Reimagining the West African Lanscape

6-Stack is a six-storey residential design proposal comprising 20 residential district of Lagos, the project explores a fundamental question: how can contemporary architecture in West Africa remain responsive to its environment while retaining a meaningful connection to the cultural landscapes from which it emerges? For Architectural Design Studio CCWorkshop, the answer lies in rejecting the conventional approach of reproducing vernacular architecture literally and treating culture as an applied aesthetic.
